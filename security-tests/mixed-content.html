A Short History of Public-Key Cryptography

For most of history, encryption was symmetric: the same secret key both locked and unlocked a message, so the two parties first had to share that key through some trusted channel. The key-distribution problem — how to agree on a secret without already having a secure way to talk — was the central obstacle to encryption at scale.

The breakthrough came in the 1970s. In 1976, Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman published a method by which two parties could establish a shared secret over a public channel, without ever transmitting the secret itself. Their key-exchange protocol relied on the difficulty of computing discrete logarithms — easy to combine values one way, hard to reverse.

Shortly after, Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman introduced RSA, the first practical public-key cryptosystem. RSA gave each participant a public key for encrypting and a private key for decrypting, resting its security on the difficulty of factoring large composite numbers.

It later emerged that researchers at GCHQ — James Ellis, Clifford Cocks, and Malcolm Williamson — had discovered equivalent techniques several years earlier, but their work was classified and went unpublished until 1997. The public reinvention is what changed the world.

Public-key cryptography underpins nearly all secure communication today: TLS for the web, signed software updates, and the certificate authorities that bind identities to keys. Its core insight — that you can publish the means to send you a secret without compromising your ability to keep one — remains one of the most consequential ideas in computer science.
